> How do you eat an elephant?
> A little bit at a time.

The idea of writing a novel or a feature film script can be overwhelming. But if you eat the elephant a little bit at a time, it’s a lot easier to stomach.

When it comes to a lengthy process like writing a book, there are a lot of steps. Planning your story, fleshing out your characters, planning your structure and arcs, writing the first draft, rewriting, sending to friends and gathering feedback, rewriting, rewriting, rewriting, editing, formatting, marketing…

## The Art of the Nibble: Why Granularity is Your Best Friend

The key is get granular. Chop up that elephant then concentrate on the first nibble. When you’ve finished that, start on the next. E.g. “Today, I’m fleshing out my characters. Tomorrow, I’m making the beat spreadsheet,” or ”Starting the Scrivener project”, whatever.

So how can we effectively break down our massive projects into easily digestible tasks?

The answer will be different for everyone and you should choose the medium that works for for you. I use Notion, but in the past I’ve used a bullet journal, a Google Sheet, and physical post-its.

First, write down what you want to achieve. Write a book. Make a film. Write a symphony. Paint the Sistine Chapel.

Break the project into wide view **Phases**.

Then, break the Phases down into **Parts**.

After that, break the Parts into **Bits**.

Those Bits will become **tasks**, and each individual task shouldn’t feel overwhelming.

## Building Your Roadmap: Phases, Parts, and Bites

Each of these is a ‘phase’. Next to the phase, I’ve put in brackets how long I expect the process to take (spoiler: it always takes longer than I plan, but that’s okay).

Inside each of these dropdown menus is the steps of that phase:

These are deliberately vague and I can always add more or remove some. Each of these tickboxes is essentially a task, and the important trait of each task is that it’s completable. As in, I know when it’s done. When I have a logline, I can tick the ‘logline’ tickbox.

## Task Mastery: Making Every Step Complet-able

If I’m particularly nervous about a certain task, like the seemingly simple task of ‘Write the first draft’, I’ll often break that into smaller tasks like:

**[ ] Write Act 1
[ ] Write Act 2A
[ ] Write Act 2B
[ ] Write Act 3**

If I’m still feeling like it’s overwhelming, I’ll get even more granular, splitting Act 1 into separate chapters, or scenes.

If you don’t like working in scenes or chapters and prefer to work in time, you can create 10-20 tasks that each say

**[ ] Write for one hour**

And assign the time tasks to however many days feels good.

Don’t worry if you have loads of tasks. Life is long. You’ve got a plenty of time and the time is gonna pass anyway.

Once you’ve got all your Bits/Tasks written down, you can organise them into a calendar, or simply keep them as tickboxes. Keep them somewhere handy and visible so you always know where you are in the process.

Because I have multiple projects in the works at any given time, prefer to keep track of things on Notion, and I have a Rollup property on each project so I can keep an eye on how much I’ve done. Seeing the percentages like this gives me a thrill.

![](../../../organise/Images/newsletters/2025/how-i-plan-my-novel-writing-process-04.webp)

Because I’ve populated my task list with *hundreds* of mini-tasks that span the entire publishing process, it takes a lot of work for my percentages to creep up, so I often only concentrate on my current phase, which I’ve keep in another database that I call my ‘On Brain’ database.

![](../../../organise/Images/newsletters/2025/how-i-plan-my-novel-writing-process-05.webp)

As you go through the process and write more long-form projects, you’ll refine your own process. Keep your phase and task list editable, so you can streamline and add tasks where you see fit.

## A cheatcode for Notion nerds

If you use Notion like me, I recommend setting up Buttons that auto-populate your databases. You can set your buttons so they create pages and auto-assign the Title property to have your current page. This makes creating the task list immediate and simple:

Once I’ve created the phases, I can create the tasks that automatically connect with the phases *and* the current book whose page I’m in.

*To be clear, this is a system that works for me. It might not work for you, which is totally groovy. This kind of system-building is addictive for me and it takes a lot of willpower to ensure it doesn’t cut into my writing time too much.*

There you have it! My process for creating a process for long-form projects. It might seem overwhelming, but remember to start small and allow your personal method develop organically over time.

If you end up with too many tasks and if feels like you’ll never finish the project, just remember

> **People often overestimate what they can do in a day, but they underestimate what they can do in a lifetime.**
